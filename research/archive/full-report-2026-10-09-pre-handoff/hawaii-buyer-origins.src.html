The short answer

  • Prices outran incomes. Oʻahu's median house price rose 80% from 2006 to 2025. In 2025 the mortgage payment on a median house took 66% of a typical Oʻahu income, 78% on Maui and 76% on Kauaʻi, up from about 40% in 2019. Prices jumped first, in 2020–22; then mortgage rates rose from about 4% (2019) to 6.6% (2025). On Oʻahu the two pushed about equally (each 14–24 points, depending on which is counted first); on Maui and Kauaʻi prices pushed harder. Rising incomes took back part of it.
  • Buyers come mostly from California. Mainland buyers make about a fifth of purchases statewide, but about half the purchase dollars on the Big Island and Kauaʻi. California is the #1 out-of-state buyer state on every island; Washington, Colorado, Oregon, Alaska, Texas and Florida follow, depending on the island.
  • Hawaiʻi's families go to Las Vegas. The state lost 31,821 more households to other states than it gained in 19 years, all of it from Oʻahu. Las Vegas (Clark County) is the #1 destination county from every island, and since 2011–14 departures there have grown fastest (+60%), ahead of Phoenix, Salt Lake City and Denver.
  • Every island is different. On Oʻahu, buyers are mostly local at every price up to $10 million, including two-thirds of $3M+ buyers. On Maui, buyers without a homeowner exemption take half or more of sales at every price, and 81–89% above $3 million. Condos are the outsiders' market: about half of neighbor-island condo buyers are from out of state. The Big Island now leads at $10M+.
  • The market has turned toward buyers. Rates are back above 7%. Maui, Kauaʻi and the Big Island have 6.5–9.6 months of supply (August 2026). Oʻahu is balanced overall at 5.2 months, but its houses have only about 3, still a seller's market. So far in 2026, Oʻahu house sales are up 6%, and Maui condo sales are up 21% at prices 7% lower.
  • The playbook. For the neighbor islands, the biggest feeder is Oʻahu. On the mainland, find buyers in the Bay Area, Los Angeles–Orange County and Seattle, and second-home buyers in Salt Lake City, Denver, Minneapolis and Anchorage. Build referral partnerships in Las Vegas, Phoenix and Texas, where departing Hawaiʻi households go.

What happened, and why

Nine chapters: what the data shows, and our read of why

2006–2007The top of the first boom
  • Oʻahu recorded 10,421 sales in 2006, a level not seen again until 2021. Maui recorded 2,331.
  • Oʻahu's price index peaked in 2006 at 95.3 (2015 = 100). Maui's single-family median averaged $702K that year.
  • Mortgage rates averaged 6.41% and 6.34%, close to 2026's year-to-date average of 6.49%. The state authorized 7,549 new homes in 2006, the most in 20 years.
  • The neighbor islands were gaining households: in 2004–05 the Big Island took in 1,126 more households from other states than it lost.
  • On Maui, 37% of 2004 home sales went to buyers living outside Maui County, and more than half in West Maui Reported (County of Maui Planning, 2006). No official statewide series by buyer origin starts before 2008.
Our read: easy credit and a national housing boom. Mainland households were moving to the neighbor islands (IRS shows net gains there), while Oʻahu was already losing slightly more households than it gained.
2008–2011The crash
  • Sales fell 40% on Oʻahu from 2006 to 2009 (to 6,263) and 34% on Maui (to 1,528).
  • Maui's single-family median fell 37%, from $702K (2006) to $439K (2011). On the same measure Oʻahu's fell 9%, from $632K to $576K; its repeat-sales index fell 14%, from 95.3 to 82.3.
  • New-home permits collapsed to 2,592 in 2009. Rates fell from 6.04% (2008) to 4.46% (2011).
  • Mainland buyers' share of recorded sales rose from 23.5% (2008) to 29.3% in 2010 (6,207 purchases), the high of the 2008–2025 series. That year recorded sales jumped 31% while MLS sales rose 19%, so read the 2010 peak with caution. Foreign buyers' share reached 5.0% in 2011.
  • On the neighbor islands, migration nearly stopped: the net flow was a few hundred households a year either way. Visitor arrivals by air bottomed at 6.42 million in 2009.
Our read: the national financial crisis. Prices fell much harder on Maui (−37% median) than on Oʻahu (−9% median, −14% repeat-sales index). Maui depends more on outside buyers and visitors, while on Oʻahu the military and government base steadied demand, even though Oʻahu's sales count fell slightly more.
2012–2015Recovery, and the foreign buyer
  • Rates hit 3.66% in 2012. Oʻahu's price index rose 21%, from 82.3 (2011) to 100 (2015).
  • International visitors rose from 2.05 million (2011) to 2.78 million (2015). Foreign buyers' share was 4.9% in 2012, then fell to 3.0% by 2015. The mainland share held near 21% in 2012–2014, then fell to 19% in 2015.
  • The neighbor islands gained households again, and the arrivals were richer. On Kauaʻi in 2012–13, households moving in averaged $103K in income, against $47K for those leaving.
Our read: cheap money, a strong yen (2011–12) and a Canadian dollar near par drew Asian and Canadian buyers. As the yen weakened from 2013, the foreign share fell. Higher-income mainland households began replacing departing locals on the neighbor islands.
2016–2019The steady climb
  • About 19,800–20,500 sales a year. Mainland buyers took 3,702–3,956 of them, between 18.6% and 19.4%.
  • Foreign purchases reached their highest count in 2018, 928 (4.5%), averaging $1.25M. Buyers with Japan addresses made 610 purchases statewide that year Reported (Title Guaranty, via Hawaii Business).
  • Mortgage rates averaged 3.65–4.54%. Visitor arrivals by air reached 10.24 million in 2019.
  • Oʻahu started losing 3,300–7,700 households a year to other states. Maui's mainland buyer share slid from 42% (2015) to 28% (2019).
Our read: record tourism and low rates kept outside demand steady. Meanwhile Oʻahu households were leaving, mostly for Las Vegas, the West Coast and Texas, which fits the cost-of-living gap shown under The squeeze on local families.
2020The shutdown
  • The 14-day travel quarantine cut air arrivals 74%, from 10.24 million to 2.68 million.
  • Foreign purchases fell 55%, to 243 from 540 a year earlier. Mainland purchases fell 14%, to 3,225 (17.4%), the lowest count and share since at least 2008.
  • Total sales dipped to 18,549. Rates fell to a 3.11% average.
Our read: the quarantine made visiting hard until pre-travel testing began on Oct 15, 2020. Outside purchases fell while locals kept buying on record-low rates, so the local share rose to 81%.
2021The rush
  • A record 25,970 sales, up 40%. Mainland purchases jumped 80% to 5,806, paying an average $1.20M.
  • Rates averaged 2.96%, the 20-year low.
  • Inventory fell by more than half: months of supply dropped to about one month on every island.
  • The Bay Area was the #2 source of new households from 2019–20 through 2021–22. The rise began just before the pandemic. Households moving to Maui in 2021–22 reported an average $192K in income, about double the usual level.
  • On Maui, buyers without a homeowner exemption (second homes, investors and landlords) peaked at 64.5% of home sales in FY2022 (July 2021–June 2022).
Our read: remote work, record-low mortgage rates of about 3% and stock-market wealth arrived together. High-income West Coast households could live and work here, and they bought faster than sellers listed.
2022–2023Rate shock and the fire
  • Rates jumped from 2.96% to 5.33% (2022) and 6.80% (2023). Recorded sales fell to 21,131, then 15,638, the fewest in the 2008–2025 recorded series. MLS sales (11,997 in 2023) stayed above their 2008–2011 levels.
  • The mainland share held at 22–23%. From 2021 to 2023, mainland purchases fell 38% and local purchases fell 41%.
  • The Lahaina fire on Aug 8, 2023 destroyed most of the town. Maui County air arrivals fell from 2.97 million (2022) to 2.53 million (2023).
Our read: higher rates froze local owners in place, because few would trade a 3% mortgage for a 7% one. Mainland purchases fell almost as much as local ones (38% vs 41%), so the mainland share held.
2024–2025The locked-in market
  • DBEDT counts 18,007 sales in 2024 and 16,909 in 2025. Its 2024 figure runs about 8% above Title Guaranty's own 2024 count (16,709), while the two agree for 2023 and 2025; on Title Guaranty's count, 2025 sales rose 1.2%. The mainland share was 19.6% in 2025, against 22.8% in 2024.
  • On Maui the mainland share fell to 25% in 2025, the lowest in the 2015–2025 county series, and condo prices fell sharply Reported, as condo insurance costs rose and Maui moved to phase out apartment-zoned vacation rentals (Bill 9, signed Dec 15, 2025, with deadlines in 2029 and 2031). Read Maui's long decline with care: the published series broke in Q3 2018, when Title Guaranty's Maui condo count fell to about 75% of MLS condo sales, while county tax records show buyers without a homeowner exemption steady at 55–57% (see the Why box below the island charts).
  • The mortgage payment on a median house took 66% of median income on Oʻahu and 78% on Maui in 2025.
Our read: rates stayed between 6.6% and 6.7% while prices held, so the monthly payment on a median house rose 75% on Oʻahu and 96% on Maui from 2021 to 2025. Oʻahu's 90-day rental minimum (2022) and Maui's pending Bill 9 made vacation-rental investing riskier, and HOA and insurance costs rose.
2026 to dateRates climb again
  • The 30-year rate rose from 6.05% (February average) to 7.28% on Oct 1, after six straight weekly increases.
  • Oʻahu's single-family median set a record of $1,242,500 in June, then fell to $1,110,000 in September, down 3.9% from a year earlier Reported.
  • Months of supply (August): Maui 9.6, Kauaʻi 8.4, Big Island 6.5, Oʻahu 5.2. That is buyers' territory on three of four islands.
  • January–June 2026, statewide: 7,738 recorded sales, up 1.5% from a year earlier. The mainland share eased to 19.2% (21.8%) while foreign buyers rose to 2.9% (2.3%). Oʻahu sales rose 9%, the Big Island fell 9% and Kauaʻi 34% Official (DBEDT quarterly report). Buyers included 552 from California, 175 from Washington and 138 from Japan Reported. MLS sales for the same months were down 1.0% (DBEDT). Two one-offs drive the island gap. Most of Oʻahu's rise is one tower: The Park Ward Village closed 527 pre-sold units in Q2 2026 (Howard Hughes), and without it Oʻahu's recorded sales fell about 2%. Kauaʻi's drop is measured against about 80 unusually cheap Līhuʻe condo transfers in Q2 2025; adjusted, it is about 18–21% Primary.
Our read: rates fell through 2025 to a 6.05% low in February 2026, then rose instead. New listings are down from a year ago on Maui, Kauaʻi and the Big Island, but homes are selling more slowly, so inventory keeps building and the advantage is moving toward buyers.

Why departing households go where they go

Departing households leave mainly because of housing costs. They land where housing is cheaper and jobs have grown, the same pull that draws movers from every state. For brokers

Keep a standing referral partner in Las Vegas, the steadiest one-way corridor. Treat base-town markets as orders-driven and seasonal: San Antonio, El Paso, Colorado Springs, Tacoma and San Diego. The interior West is smaller (about 180–360 households a year to Denver and Salt Lake City) and runs both ways. When pitching a move, lead with the housing payment gap, not taxes.

For local families

The cheapest destinations also tend to have the most volatile job markets. The flow to Las Vegas fell 38% when Nevada's economy collapsed in 2008–10. Coming back is hard once Hawaiʻi prices move on. If you might return, weigh renting out your home instead of selling it, keeping in mind that you would lose the homeowner exemption.

The checked answer

Hawaii's departing households leave mainly because housing costs too much, and they land where housing is cheaper and jobs have grown. That mostly follows the same pull that draws movers from every state. Las Vegas is the one place Hawaii residents favor far more than other Americans do. The push is cost. In August 2026 a typical Honolulu asking rent ran about $3,020 a month, roughly $1,300 more than in Las Vegas or Phoenix. For a single-family buyer the payment gap is about $3,700 a month. O'ahu households that moved to another state reported less income than those who stayed ($70k vs $90k average AGI, 2022–23). From 2011–14 to 2019–23, Hawaii's moves to Colorado, Washington, Arizona, Oregon and Idaho grew no faster than all U.S. moves into those states. Over the same period Hawaii's moves to Nevada grew 54%, against 19% nationally. Las Vegas is also the only strongly one-way corridor: about 1,650 households a year leave for it, roughly 2.4 for every one that comes back. That fits a 'ninth island' community built since Boyd Gaming began marketing to Hawaii in the 1970s, and those who go have lower incomes, which fits a cost-driven move. The flow still depends on jobs there: it fell 38% when Nevada's economy collapsed in 2008–10. Military orders are a separate channel. Counties dominated by bases take about 9% of O'ahu's leavers, up to about 18% if Tacoma, San Antonio and El Paso are counted, and these moves explain roughly half of the departures to Colorado (Colorado Springs) and Texas. State income tax matters little, about $1,600–3,400 a year at typical leaver incomes, and flows to no-tax states grew no faster than flows to taxed ones. Calling the interior West the 'fastest-growing exit' holds only against a mid-2000s base. Since 2011, Las Vegas (+60%) and Phoenix (+36%) grew faster than Salt Lake City (+35%) and Denver (+30%). Only Utah, mainly Provo, shows a Hawaii-specific lift. Those corridors are small, about 180–360 households a year, and Salt Lake City departures have fallen since 2020–21. The IRS data stop in early 2023, so later events are not reflected. For brokers, Las Vegas is the corridor worth a standing referral partner, and base-town markets are an orders-driven, seasonal business; lead with the housing-cost gap, not taxes. For families, the cheapest destinations, Las Vegas above all, also have the most volatile job markets.

Each driver, as checked

  • The housing-cost gap (strong; supported by the check). High housing costs are the main reason households leave, and every top destination is much cheaper. At August 2026 asking rents, a Honolulu renter pays about $1,300 a month more than in Las Vegas or Phoenix (about $15,000 a year). For a single-family buyer the payment gap is about $3,700 a month. The gap does not explain which corridors grew or when. Las Vegas departures bottomed in 2009–12, when its homes were cheapest relative to Hawaii, and peaked after 2019, when its prices were rising faster than Honolulu's.
  • Las Vegas, the 'ninth island': community, decades of casino marketing and frequent flights (strong; supported by the check). Las Vegas is the one destination Hawaii residents favor far more than other Americans do. From 2011–14 to 2019–23, Hawaii's moves to Nevada grew 54% while all U.S. moves into Nevada grew 19%, and about 2.4 households leave for Las Vegas for each one that comes back. Its lower costs fit the lower incomes of those who go (about $64k average AGI). The pull is reinforced by a Hawaii community dating to Boyd Gaming's 1970s marketing and by heavy visitor traffic. The flow still depends on Las Vegas jobs: it fell 38% when Nevada's job market collapsed in 2008–10.
  • Military orders: about 9–18% of Oʻahu's leavers go to counties dominated by bases (strong; supported by the check). Military orders are a separate channel that people do not choose. Counties dominated by bases take about 9% of O'ahu's departing households, rising to about 18% if mixed civilian-military metros such as Tacoma, San Antonio and El Paso are included, and more if San Diego is counted. Military moves make up about half of departures to Colorado (Colorado Springs) and to Texas (San Antonio, Killeen, El Paso), which inflates the apparent growth of those 'exits'. The neighbor islands send almost none.
  • Jobs: leavers follow the same job growth as other Americans (strong; supported by the check). Destinations that added jobs fastest also drew the fastest-growing share of Hawaii's leavers, but that is the national migration pattern, not something special about Hawaii. Outside Nevada, and to a lesser degree Utah, Hawaii's flows grew no faster than everyone else's moves into those states. On the Hawaii side, UHERO finds high prices and persistent local constraints drive departures from Honolulu more than low incomes, although income is now adding to the pressure.
  • Family and community networks: real for Las Vegas and Utah County (weak; weakened by the check). Community ties clearly help explain Las Vegas and probably the Utah County (Provo) corridor, where Hawaii's moves grew faster than the national flow. There is no evidence that networks drive moves to Denver or Boise: Hawaii's flows there grew no faster than everyone else's, and the growth of communities with Hawaiʻi roots by state does not line up with where Hawaii's leavers went.
  • State income tax: about $1,600–3,400 a year, small next to housing costs (weak; supported by the check). State income tax is a minor factor. At a typical leaver's income it is worth about $1,600–3,400 a year, against a housing-cost gap of about $15,000 a year in rent (more for owners). Hawaii's flows to no-tax states grew no faster than flows to states with income taxes, and Hawaii's 2026 standard-deduction increase narrows the gap further.

Why

Why Maui’s mainland buyer share fell

Most of Maui's fall from about 52% mainland buyers in 2010 to 25% in 2025 is not a mainland exodus. First, 2010 was an abnormal starting year, full of bust-era resort-condo closings. The checked answer

Canadians are the top foreign buyers on Maui and Hawaii Island mainly because foreign buying there is small, and Japan's much larger buyer base (213 purchases statewide in 2025) is almost entirely on Oahu, where every Japan flight lands. Canada's winter visitors, by contrast, lean toward Maui and Kona. In 2025, 40.8% of Hawaii's 389,241 Canadian visitors went to Maui, compared with about 26% of all visitors. Two-thirds of them had been before, and about 1,600 Maui County properties already send their tax bills to Canada, many of them South Maui condos. Canada has led foreign purchases by count on both islands in every year checked since 2015, though on Hawaii Island in 2025 Japan slightly beat it by dollar value.

The 2025 drop, from 106 to 64 purchases ($186.0M to $110.7M), happened on the neighbor islands. Maui County went from 39 to 11, Hawaii Island from 33 to 17 and Kauai from 3 to 1. Oahu rose from 31 to 35 only because of a one-time cluster of 22 Canadian purchases in early 2025. Statewide, Canadian closings were flat in January to March 2025 (31 vs 32), and the whole decline came from April to December.

Two explanations fit the data best:

  • A Maui property-market shock. Almost all of Maui's drop was Canadians in South Maui (33 to 6). In the first half of 2025, South Maui sales fell for every kind of buyer, including locals (256 to 147 sales). That coincided with the Bill 9 plan to end vacation rentals in apartment-zoned condos (first proposed in May 2024, with much nearer deadlines than the final law), higher property-tax rates on short-term rentals and falling condo prices. The data show the shock but cannot pin it on Bill 9 alone.

Canadian trips to the US fell 23.5% in 2025, and in an August 2025 poll 54% of Canadian owners of US property said they planned to sell. Canadian buying on Maui stayed low in late 2025 and 2026 even after South Maui and Maui condo sales recovered for other buyers.

The pullback shows up in willingness to own a home in the US, not in visitor numbers. Canadian visitors to Maui fell only 4.4% while Canadian purchases there fell 72%.

  • Weak Canadian dollar: background only. It weakened just 2% on average in 2025, its January 2025 low was partly caused by the tariff threat, and purchases did not recover in early 2026 when it was about 2% stronger.

  • US tax rules: the one lasting 2025 change, a larger estate-tax exemption from 2026, actually favors Canadians.
  • WestJet Lihue cancellation: starts in December 2026, on an island where Canadians bought 1 to 3 homes a year, so it explains none of the 2025 drop.

For brokers

Confirm a unit's legal rental status in writing before marketing it as an income property, and test every vacation-rental deal against long-term rent. On Oʻahu's ʻEwa, Waiʻanae and Wahiawā sides, 36–44% of mortgaged buyers use VA loans. Make those listings VA-ready, check condo project approval, and time launches for the summer military move season.

For local families

On Oʻahu, non-military families compete with buyers who can put zero down and borrow at about half a point less, most of all in the ʻEwa, Waiʻanae and Wahiawā areas. On the neighbor islands, investor competition is concentrated in resort areas. In Wailuku, Kahului and Makawao the share of buyers who won't live in the home is close to Oʻahu's. State and county first-time-buyer programs can help close the down-payment gap.

The checked answer

In 2025 mortgage records (HMDA), investment-property loans were 30.7% of home-purchase loans on Kauai, 24.6% on Maui and 22.8% on Hawaii Island, against 12.7% on Oahu. VA loans were 27.1% of Oahu purchase loans, against 5–11% on the other islands. The investor gap mostly follows where vacation renting happens, not the island line. Across 47 ZIP codes, the share of homes used as short-term rentals explains nearly two-thirds of the variation in investor share, and once it is accounted for, being on Oahu makes no difference. Resort areas (Lahaina, Kihei, Princeville, Poipu, Kona, Waikoloa, Waikiki) run 29–54% investor loans; non-resort towns on every island (Kahului, Wailuku, Mililani, Ewa, Waipahu) run 3–12%. Resort amenities and past investor buying are tangled up with zoning, so this is an association, not proof that zoning alone causes it. The military answers the VA question but explains only a small part of the investor gap. About 43,700 active-duty members and 44,100 dependents live on Oahu, against fewer than 900 people combined on all other islands. VA's terms make it their default loan: no down payment, no mortgage insurance, no loan cap with full entitlement, looser debt limits and rates about half a point lower. Honolulu's VA share is normal for a U.S. military town: 24.7% of 2025 purchase applications, about the same as Anchorage (24.6%) and below Virginia Beach (37.5%) and Colorado Springs' El Paso County (35.4%). Taking VA loans out lifts Oahu's investor share only to 17.2%, which closes 2–3 of the 10–18 points. Three cautions matter. First, the 'investor' label shifted. Since 2019, lenders have recoded vacation-home loans from 'second home' to 'investment', and lenders that don't verify income grew from about 1% to 4–10% of purchase loans. The combined second-home-plus-investor share barely moved: 14–16% on Oahu and 30–40% on the neighbor islands. Second, Hawaii Island is a different story. Its investor-only share equaled Oahu's in 2019 (10.5% vs 10.3%). Much of its 2025 gap sits in Puna, where $250K–450K homes can pay for themselves as long-term rentals, and one bridge lender charging about 10.5% made 77 of the county's 405 investor loans. Third, HMDA leaves out cash buyers. On Maui, 57% of FY2025 home sales went to buyers not eligible for the homeowner exemption, against 34% of mortgaged buyers, so the real gap with Oahu is probably wider. Oahu's 90-day rental minimum, military housing allowances and conforming loan limits explain little of the difference between islands.

Each driver, as checked

  • Where vacation rentals are legal (strong; supported by the check). Across 47 ZIP codes, the share of homes used as short-term rentals explains nearly two-thirds of the variation in investor share of mortgaged purchases. Once it is accounted for, being on Oahu makes no difference. Resort ZIPs run 29–54% investor (Lahaina, Kihei, Princeville, Koloa/Poipu, Kona, Waikoloa, Waikiki), while non-resort towns on every island run 3–12% (Kahului, Wailuku, Kalaheo, Mililani, Ewa, Waipahu). Exceptions are Puna and Hilo on Hawaii Island (19–48%) and Ala Moana/Ward on Oahu (29.5%), where investor buying is not tied to STRs. Because STR stock partly reflects past investor buying and resort amenities, this shows association with vacation-rental geography, not a clean zoning effect.
  • Oʻahu's military population (strong; supported by the check). VA loans are 27% of Oahu purchase loans because about 43,700 active-duty members and 44,100 dependents live on Oahu, against fewer than 900 people combined on all other islands. Honolulu's VA share (24.7% of 2025 purchase applications) is typical of U.S. military-host counties. It is in line with Anchorage (24.6%) and below Virginia Beach (37.5%) and El Paso County, Colorado (35.4%). These owner-occupant VA buyers dilute Oahu's investor share only modestly. Removing them lifts it from 12.7% to 17.2%, which closes 2–3 of the 10–18 point gap.
  • VA loan terms: the cheapest way to buy for those eligible (strong; supported by the check). For eligible buyers, VA is the cheapest way to buy on Oahu. Of VA loans that report a loan-to-value ratio, 61% financed 100% or more of the value. The median VA rate was 5.875% vs 6.375% for conventional owner-occupant loans. 47% of VA borrowers had debt-to-income of 50% or more, vs 1% of conventional ones. VA also finances above the conforming limit with no down payment: 172 of 436 Oahu loans above $1,209,750 in 2025 were VA. VA's share of owner-occupant purchases holds at 31–35% from under $500K up to $1.5M.
  • How lenders label loans: vacation homes recoded from 'second home' to 'investment' (moderate; supported by the check). Part of the 23–31% investor figure is a labeling and lending effect. Since 2019, lenders moved vacation-home loans from 'second home' to 'investment', so the combined non-owner share barely changed (Oahu 14–16%; neighbor islands 30–40%). Meanwhile, lenders that don't verify income grew from about 1% to 4–10% of all purchase loans. Without those loans, 2025 investor shares are Kauai 21%, Maui 19%, Hawaii Island 13% and Oahu 9%. On Hawaii Island, one bridge lender charging about 10.5% made 77 of 405 investor loans, mostly in Puna.
  • Military rotations and housing allowances keep VA buyers coming (weak; weakened by the check). Steady military turnover keeps feeding VA buyers. 22% of people who moved to Hawaii from another state in the past year were active duty (ACS 2024). 2026 housing allowances for mid-grade families ($3,663–$4,428) roughly match principal and interest on a median Oahu VA loan, before taxes, insurance and HOA dues. Because the allowance is set at local rents, buying is not clearly cheaper than renting each month. The appeal is zero-down equity. Allowance levels do not explain the difference between islands.
  • Long-term rents rarely cover a financed payment, except in Puna (weak; supported by the check). Long-term rental demand does not explain Oahu's low investor share. At Oahu prices, a 70%-financed condo or house does not cover its payment from rent, and investor shares in Oahu suburbs are 3–6%. The exception is Puna on Hawaii Island. There, $250K–450K homes rent for $1,350–1,750, gross yields reach 5–8%, and investor loans are 17–25% of purchases despite few legal vacation rentals.
  • Oʻahu's 90-day rental minimum is not the reason (weak; supported by the check). Oahu's 90-day minimum is not why Oahu's investor share is low. The share was already 10.3% in 2019, and the investor hot spot, Waikiki, is resort-zoned and unaffected. Outside lenders that don't verify income, Oahu's investor share actually slipped from 9.3% to 8.8% between 2019 and 2025.
  • Loan limits are the same on every island (weak; supported by the check). Conforming loan limits were the same on every island in 2025 ($1,209,750), so they do not explain the investor gap. They matter on Oahu only because VA buyers with full entitlement can borrow above the limit with no down payment. 172 of the 436 Oahu purchase loans above the limit in 2025 were VA.

Why

Why a home now takes so much more of a local paycheck

Two shocks hit in sequence. First, prices jumped in 2020–22, while rates were near 3% and listings had nearly vanished. Then mortgage rates averaged 6.6% in 2025, up from 3.9% in 2019, which raised the payment on every borrowed dollar by 35%, and prices did not fall back. On Oʻahu the two pushed about equally: each added 14–24 points, depending on which is counted first. On Maui and Kauaʻi prices pushed harder, adding 30–41 points against 13–25 for rates. Rising incomes took back part of it. Hawaiʻi's chronic limits explain why the starting point was already about 40%: slow permits, little urban land and high building costs.

For brokers

Price buyers on payment, not price: each point of mortgage rate moves the payment about 10–11%. At the October 8 rate of 7.40%, the report's formula gives about 75% of income on Oʻahu, 82% on Maui and 89% on Kauaʻi. For any condo listing, collect the master insurance policy and deductible, the reserve study and any planned special assessments up front. They now decide whether a deal can be financed.

For local families

The headline figure compares one median house with one median household. It leaves out taxes, insurance and condo dues, so treat it as a gauge, not a budget. Rates matter most now: a one-point drop is worth roughly a 10% price cut. But prices also rose faster than pay in 2020–22, so lower rates alone won't bring back 2019 conditions. Rules of the road

ZIP-level precision lives in the mail, not the ad platforms

Meta (FB/IG)
Housing ads go under the special ad category. No targeting by ZIP code, age or gender: audiences must include ages 18–65+ and all genders, and a city or address expands to at least a 15-mile radius in the U.S. and Canada (15 km elsewhere). No location exclusions or lookalike audiences. You can target origin cities and states, and use your own client list if Meta marks it eligible.
Google
Under the housing policy (U.S. and Canada), there is no targeting by ZIP, age, gender, parental status or marital status. Use city, metro, state or radius.
Fair housing
Ads that indicate a preference based on national origin, ancestry, race or other protected classes are illegal (42 USC 3604(c); HRS §515-3), and targeting by place can act as a proxy for them. Base geographic targeting on documented demand, keep it broad, and have country-level or language-specific campaigns reviewed. Keep ad copy about the property, never about the buyer. HUD withdrew its 2024 guidance on digital housing ads in September 2025, but the law itself is unchanged.
Direct mail
Can go to any ZIP except 96761, 96767 and 96790 (see Lahaina and Kula). Owner mailing addresses in county tax records are public under Hawaiʻi's records law (OIP Op. Ltr. 11-01); Maui County offers a free owner and mailing-address extract. Fair-housing and brokerage-name rules still apply.
Referrals
Referral fees to out-of-state agents must go through licensed brokerages, under each state's licensing rules.
Every ad
HAR §16-99-11 requires the brokerage's legal or registered trade name and the licensee's status.
Lahaina and Kula
The governor's wildfire emergency proclamations ban unsolicited offers to buy any real property from any owner in ZIP codes 96761, 96767 and 96790 (Lahaina–Kāʻanapali–Kapalua, Lahaina post office boxes, and Kula). A violation is a misdemeanor: up to a $5,000 fine and a year in jail. The ban has been renewed in every proclamation since August 2023; the latest checked, the Thirty-second (August 24, 2026), runs about 60 days, to around October 23, 2026. Check for a renewal before relying on its lapse, and do not prospect there.

A summary of rules, not legal advice. Confirm campaigns with your principal broker.
